# Orion Foods: Capital Structure Recommendation

## Corporate Finance Case Analysis

### Executive recommendation

**Recommendation: adopt Option B, with total debt of €1.0 billion and debt equal to 50% of total capital.** This structure generates a meaningful annual interest tax shield of €15 million while maintaining interest coverage of 3.5× in the modeled recession, above the board’s 2.0× warning threshold. It is also broadly consistent with the peer leverage range of 38%–55%.

Option A preserves the most financial flexibility but appears unnecessarily conservative given Orion’s stable cash flows, tangible assets and current free-cash-flow agency concerns. Option C maximizes the tax shield, but recession coverage falls to 1.56× and leverage may impair future investment capacity and distort shareholder incentives. Because the case provides no probability-weighted distress costs, Option B is a reasoned recommendation rather than a mathematically exact optimum.

## 1. Company context and decision

Orion Foods is a publicly listed European producer of packaged food. It operates in a mature industry with historically stable demand, although sales and margins decline during economic downturns. The company owns most of its manufacturing facilities and distribution centers, giving it a substantial tangible-asset base.

In the latest financial year, Orion generated €2.5 billion in revenue and €300 million in EBIT. It has €500 million of existing debt at an average rate of 5%, implying €25 million of annual interest expense. Its market value of equity is €1.5 billion, so current debt / total capital is 25%. The corporate tax rate is 25%, and Orion expects to have sufficient taxable income to use interest deductions fully.

The proposed recapitalizations use any debt raised above the existing €500 million to repurchase shares. They change the financing mix, not operating assets or EBIT. The three operating scenarios are €360 million of EBIT in a strong year, €300 million in a normal year and €210 million in a recession. The board considers recession interest coverage below 2.0× a significant financial risk.

A possible €200 million automated production facility may become available in approximately two years. The project is not approved and may never proceed, but it is expected to have a positive NPV of €30 million if market conditions develop as expected.

## 2. Quantitative analysis

The annual interest tax shield is calculated as interest expense multiplied by the 25% corporate tax rate. Interest coverage is calculated as EBIT divided by annual interest expense.

| Measure | Option A | Option B | Option C |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Total debt | €500m | €1,000m | €1,500m |
| Debt / total capital | 25% | 50% | 75% |
| Average interest rate | 5.0% | 6.0% | 9.0% |
| Annual interest expense | €25m | €60m | €135m |
| **Annual interest tax shield** | **€6.25m** | **€15.00m** | **€33.75m** |
| Interest coverage — strong year (EBIT €360m) | 14.40× | 6.00× | 2.67× |
| Interest coverage — normal year (EBIT €300m) | 12.00× | 5.00× | 2.22× |
| Interest coverage — recession (EBIT €210m) | 8.40× | 3.50× | **1.56×** |

The tax shield increases with leverage: Option B generates €8.75 million more per year than A; Option C generates €18.75 million more than B. However, the shield is a tax benefit, not a complete measure of the value created by debt. Interest obligations, expected financial distress, reduced flexibility and agency effects must also be considered.

Option C breaches the board’s recession threshold: coverage of 1.56× is below 2.0×. Option B retains 3.50× coverage in the same scenario. Its normal-year coverage of 5.00× is also within the comparable-company range of 4.4×–7.2×.

## 3. Debt capacity and peer evidence

### Factors supporting additional debt

Orion’s mature operations, historically stable demand, consistent profitability and tangible assets support debt capacity. Its taxable income is expected to be sufficient to use the interest deductions. There are no major debt maturities or committed acquisitions in the next three years, and normal EBIT covers Option B’s interest expense five times.

### Factors limiting additional debt

Sales and margins decline in downturns while interest obligations remain fixed. The board explicitly regards coverage below 2.0× in a recession as significant risk. Higher leverage also increases Orion’s borrowing rate under the case assumptions and reduces its capacity to respond to unexpected shocks or future investment opportunities.

### Comparable companies

The peer group reports debt / total capital ratios from 38% to 55%, with interest coverage between 4.4× and 7.2×. The simple peer averages are 47% leverage and approximately 5.7× coverage. Option B’s 50% leverage is close to the peer average, and its normal-year coverage of 5.0× lies within the observed range. This evidence supports B as plausible, but peer averages should be treated as benchmarks rather than targets. Orion’s recession exposure and potential investment needs remain company-specific.

## 4. Capital structure, incentives and investment

### Tax benefits and the trade-off theory

Interest deductibility creates a tax shield, consistent with the tax benefit of debt in the Modigliani–Miller framework with corporate taxes. In practice, a capital structure decision must also account for expected distress costs and other costs of leverage. The trade-off theory therefore supports increasing debt only while the marginal tax benefit remains worth the associated risk and loss of flexibility.

### Free-cash-flow discipline

Orion has retained substantial free cash flow and invested some of it in diversification projects with returns below those of its core operations. Moderate debt service can reduce discretionary cash available for low-return expansion and impose discipline on management. Because the proceeds above existing debt are used for a share repurchase, the recapitalization also distributes cash to shareholders while committing the company to future interest payments.

### Debt overhang and the possible plant

The future plant requires €200 million and is expected to have a positive NPV of €30 million, but it is unapproved and uncertain. Excessive leverage today could make Orion unable or unwilling to finance a positive-NPV project later. When new investment partly benefits existing creditors by making their claims safer, shareholders may have weak incentives to provide additional equity. This is the debt-overhang or underinvestment problem. The plant therefore supports preserving reasonable financing headroom, but its uncertainty does not justify raising €200 million today.

### Risk shifting and asset substitution

At very high leverage, shareholders may prefer projects with unusually high upside and substantial downside. Shareholders retain much of the upside, while creditors bear part of the downside through a higher probability of default. This conflict is known as risk shifting or asset substitution. Option C creates a stronger basis for this conflict than Option B.

## 5. Comparison of alternatives

| Criterion | Option A | Option B | Option C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main benefit | Lowest financial risk and greatest headroom | Balanced tax shield, discipline and resilience | Largest annual tax shield |
| Main cost / risk | Limited incremental tax benefit; weaker cash-flow discipline | Higher obligations and less flexibility than A | 1.56× recession coverage; elevated distress and incentive risk |
| Tax benefit | Low: €6.25m per year | Medium: €15.00m per year | High: €33.75m per year |
| Financial risk | Low | Moderate and manageable in the modeled scenarios | High; below the 2.0× recession threshold |
| Financial flexibility | High | Medium | Low |
| Investment incentives | Less debt discipline; low-return spending may continue | Discipline without extreme leverage | Greater debt-overhang and risk-shifting concerns |

## 6. Recommendation and implementation considerations

Option B best balances the tax benefits of debt with recession resilience, future investment capacity and managerial incentives. It raises the annual tax shield to €15 million and lowers discretionary free cash flow, while keeping recession coverage at 3.5×. The 50% debt-to-capital ratio is within the peer range and close to its 47% average.

Option A remains a defensible choice if management places exceptionally high value on financial flexibility or expects materially worse downturns than the case’s recession scenario. However, on the facts provided, it leaves significant borrowing capacity unused and does less to address low-return discretionary investment.

Option C is not recommended. The additional €18.75 million annual tax shield over B does not adequately compensate for coverage falling below the board’s threshold in a recession, substantially reduced flexibility and stronger agency conflicts. Management should reassess the capital structure if the plant is approved, operating conditions deteriorate or forecasts change materially. The case does not provide enough information to quantify an exact optimal leverage ratio or probability-weighted expected distress cost.

## Conclusion

A moderate increase in leverage is justified, but maximum leverage is not. **Option B is the most supportable recommendation** because it captures additional tax benefits and free-cash-flow discipline while retaining a meaningful recession coverage cushion and capacity for future investment.
