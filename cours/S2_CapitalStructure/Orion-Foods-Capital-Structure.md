# Orion Foods — Finding the Right Capital Structure

**Study brief and presentation draft | Corporate Finance**
**Recommendation: Option B — €1.0bn debt, 50% debt / total capital**

> This is a worked study draft based on the Orion Foods case handout. Adapt the wording and final recommendation to your group’s own discussion and course terminology.

## 1. The case in plain language

Orion is a mature packaged-food producer. Demand and cash flow are relatively stable, it owns substantial tangible assets, and it is consistently profitable. Those features support some borrowing and make the interest tax shield usable. But sales and margins still decline in recessions.

The company has €500m of debt costing 5%, so annual interest is €25m. Its market-value equity is €1.5bn, giving current debt / total capital of 25%. EBIT is €300m in a normal year, €360m in a strong year and €210m in a recession. The corporate tax rate is 25%; the case says Orion expects enough taxable income to use deductions fully.

The board is weighing more borrowing and a share repurchase. The operating assets and EBIT do not change across options; only the financing mix changes. Moderate debt could create tax savings and discipline management’s use of free cash flow. Too much debt could make recession distress more likely, reduce future financing capacity and distort investment incentives.

A €200m automated plant may become available in about two years. It is expected to have positive NPV of €30m, but it is unapproved and may never proceed. It is a reason to preserve some capacity—not a committed project that automatically rules out debt.

## 2. Part 1 — calculations

### Formulas

- **Annual interest expense** = debt × average interest rate.
- **Annual interest tax shield** = interest expense × corporate tax rate.
- **Interest coverage** = EBIT / annual interest expense.
- The board’s stated recession warning threshold is **coverage below 2.0×**.

### Results

|  | Option A | Option B | Option C |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Total debt | €500m | €1,000m | €1,500m |
| Debt / total capital | 25% | 50% | 75% |
| Average interest rate | 5.0% | 6.0% | 9.0% |
| Annual interest expense | €25m | €60m | €135m |
| **Annual tax shield at 25%** | **€6.25m** | **€15.00m** | **€33.75m** |
| Coverage — strong year (EBIT €360m) | 14.40× | 6.00× | 2.67× |
| Coverage — normal year (EBIT €300m) | 12.00× | 5.00× | 2.22× |
| Coverage — recession (EBIT €210m) | 8.40× | 3.50× | **1.56×** |

**Interpretation:** The shield increases as debt increases, but the coverage cushion falls sharply. Option C breaches the board’s 2.0× recession threshold. Option B remains at 3.5× even in the recession case.

Incremental annual tax shield: A→B = **€8.75m**; B→C = **€18.75m**; A→C = **€27.50m**. These are tax savings, **not** the total net value created by debt. Incremental interest expense and expected distress / incentive costs also matter.

## 3. Part 2 — evaluate the financing alternatives

### 1. What increases or reduces Orion’s debt capacity?

**Supports debt:** mature business; historically stable demand; predictable operating cash flow; substantial tangible assets; consistent profitability; sufficient taxable income to use deductions; no major debt maturity or committed acquisition in the next three years; normal EBIT of €300m covers Option B interest five times.

**Limits debt:** margins and sales fall in downturns; fixed interest remains due when EBIT drops; the board explicitly considers recession coverage below 2.0× a significant risk; the possible plant investment could need €200m; higher leverage raises borrowing rates in this case and reduces room to respond to shocks.

### 2. Tax benefits versus deteriorating coverage

Option B adds €8.75m of annual tax shield over A while normal-year coverage declines from 12.0× to 5.0× and recession coverage from 8.4× to 3.5×. That is a meaningful but still manageable trade-off against the stated threshold.

Option C adds another €18.75m of annual shield over B, but recession coverage falls to 1.56×, below the board’s threshold; normal coverage is only 2.22×. Without probability-weighted distress costs, the case does not permit an exact net-value calculation. Still, the downside indicator makes C too aggressive.

### 3. What do the comparable companies tell us?

Peer debt / total capital ranges from **38% to 55%**; peer interest coverage ranges from **4.4× to 7.2×**. Option B’s 50% leverage sits within the peer leverage range and its normal-year 5.0× coverage is within the peer coverage range. This supports B as plausible—not as an automatic target. Orion must account for its own recession sensitivity and investment plans. Option A at 25% is more conservative than the peers; C at 75% is well above their leverage range.

### 4. Which alternative is most appropriate?

**Recommend Option B.** It captures an additional tax shield and can impose some discipline on free cash flow, while maintaining 3.5× coverage in the modeled recession and remaining near peer capital structures. Option A leaves more flexibility but appears unnecessarily conservative given Orion’s stable cash flows and assets. Option C maximizes the tax shield but violates the recession-risk threshold and creates unacceptable costs to flexibility and incentives.

## 4. Part 3 — incentives and future investment

### Free-cash-flow discipline

Orion retains substantial cash and has invested some in diversification projects earning below its normal operating return. Moderate debt service reduces discretionary cash available for low-return expansion and can discipline management. This is the **free-cash-flow agency benefit** of debt.

### Future plant and debt overhang

The €200m automated facility is not approved, but its expected NPV is +€30m if assumptions hold. If Orion borrows too heavily today, it could later be unable or unwilling to fund a positive-NPV project. Some project value may accrue to existing creditors by making their claims safer; shareholders may then be reluctant to contribute new equity. This is the **debt-overhang / underinvestment problem**. Option B leaves more room than C. The project’s uncertainty means Orion should preserve reasonable capacity, not raise €200m today automatically.

### Risk shifting / asset substitution

With very high leverage, shareholders have limited downside beyond their equity but retain much of the upside from an unusually risky project. They may therefore prefer more risk than creditors would. Creditors bear part of the downside through a higher probability of default. This conflict is **risk shifting** (also called **asset substitution**). Leverage can discipline management at moderate levels but distort choices when excessive.

## 5. Part 4 — financial flexibility and theory

- **Option A** gives the most room to borrow later and absorb a downturn; **Option C** gives the least; **Option B** is a middle ground.
- Maximum flexibility is not automatically optimal: it can mean forgoing useful tax savings and leaving too much discretionary cash for weak projects.
- The possible plant matters as an option to invest in the future. It supports retaining headroom, especially against Option C, but it is not yet committed and should not alone dictate today’s financing choice.
- **Trade-off theory** is the main framework: balance the interest tax shield against expected distress and other debt costs.
- **Agency theory** is also directly relevant: moderate debt can reduce free-cash-flow waste; excessive debt can create underinvestment and risk shifting.
- Use other theories (for example, pecking order) only if they were covered in Sessions 1–2 and the group can connect them directly to the facts. Do not add theory just to name it.

## 6. Part 5 — comparison table

|---|---|---|---|
| Main benefit | Lowest financial risk; maximum headroom | Balanced tax shield, discipline and resilience | Largest annual tax shield |
| Main cost / risk | Little added tax benefit; weak discipline of excess cash | Higher fixed obligations and less flexibility than A | Recession coverage 1.56×; distress and incentive risks |
| Tax benefit | Low (€6.25m / year) | Medium (€15m / year) | High (€33.75m / year) |
| Financial risk | Low | Moderate / manageable in case scenarios | High; below the 2.0× recession threshold |

## 7. Part 6 — three-minute recommendation (English)

> **Our recommendation is Option B: €1.0 billion of debt, representing 50% of total capital.** It is the best balance between the tax benefits of debt and the financial and incentive costs of excessive leverage.
>
> Orion’s stable cash flows, tangible assets and consistent profitability support additional borrowing. Its interest deductions are expected to be fully usable. Option B produces an annual interest tax shield of €15 million, compared with €6.25 million under Option A. It also gives management less discretionary free cash flow, which may discourage further investment in low-return diversification projects.
>
> The tax shield should not be considered in isolation. Option B has annual interest expense of €60 million and interest coverage of 5.0 times in a normal year and 3.5 times in the modeled recession. That remains above the board’s 2.0-times recession threshold. Its 50% debt-to-capital ratio is also within the 38%–55% peer range, although peers are a benchmark rather than a target.
>
> We do not recommend Option C. Its €33.75 million tax shield is the largest, but coverage falls to 1.56 times in a recession, below the board’s stated risk threshold. This leverage would also reduce Orion’s ability to respond to a downturn or finance the possible €200 million plant. If that project still has a positive NPV of €30 million, excessive debt could create debt overhang and underinvestment. High leverage could also encourage shareholders to favor riskier projects because creditors bear part of the downside.
>
> Option A is safer and preserves the most flexibility, but it forgoes more tax benefits and may not address management’s use of free cash flow. **Option B therefore best reflects the trade-off between tax shields, distress risk, financial flexibility and agency costs.** We would monitor recession coverage and revisit the choice if the plant becomes approved or operating conditions deteriorate.

## Quick oral summary

**Choose B.** It provides a meaningful tax shield and moderate management discipline; it keeps recession coverage at 3.5×, above the 2.0× warning line; and it preserves more future financing capacity than C. A is very safe but conservative. C’s extra shield does not justify the 1.56× recession coverage, lower flexibility, debt-overhang and risk-shifting concerns.
